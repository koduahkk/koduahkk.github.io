US Electricity Overview, 2001–2023

Twenty-three years of US electricity generation by state and source, reduced to the three questions policy and leadership readers actually ask: where the power comes from, how much of it is renewable, and where the emissions sit.

Role
Solo analyst: data prep, modelling, design
Type
Self-directed project
Tools
Power BI · DAX · Power Query
Dataset
23 years · 50 states + DC · by source
01 —The question

What did the business need to know?

Is the US grid getting cleaner, and where is the progress actually happening?

National electricity figures get quoted constantly, but a single national number hides very different stories underneath. The question: which states drive US generation and emissions, and how much real progress has renewable energy made?

02 —The data

What I had to work with.

Yearly generation and emissions tables by state and energy source from the US Energy Information Administration (EIA), combined into one model.

03 —The approach

From raw tables to one trusted model.

  1. Step 01

    Combine & clean

    Appended the yearly generation and emissions tables in Power Query, standardised state names and energy-source categories, and removed duplicate and incomplete rows.

    Power Query: standardise sources+ 
    // Power Query (M): normalise source names, flag renewables
let
    Source  = Table.Combine({Gen2001_2012, Gen2013_2023}),
    Trimmed = Table.TransformColumns(Source, {{"energy_source", Text.Trim}}),
    Deduped = Table.Distinct(Trimmed, {"year", "state", "energy_source"}),
    Flagged = Table.AddColumn(Deduped, "is_renewable", each
        List.Contains({"Hydroelectric", "Wind", "Solar", "Geothermal", "Wood", "Other Biomass"}, [energy_source]))
in
    Flagged
  2. Step 02

    Model as a star schema

    Date, State and Energy Source dimensions around one fact table, so every measure responds correctly to every slicer.

  3. Step 03

    Write the measures

    DAX for renewable share, year-over-year change and state rankings.

    DAX: renewable share & YoY+ 
    Renewable Share =
DIVIDE (
    CALCULATE ( SUM ( Generation[generation_gwh] ), Source[is_renewable] = TRUE () ),
    SUM ( Generation[generation_gwh] )
)

YoY Change % =
VAR Curr = SUM ( Generation[generation_gwh] )
VAR Prev = CALCULATE ( SUM ( Generation[generation_gwh] ), DATEADD ( 'Date'[Date], -1, YEAR ) )
RETURN DIVIDE ( Curr - Prev, Prev )
  4. Step 04

    Design for the reader

    KPI cards first, ranked bars for state comparisons, a trend line for growth and a filled map for emissions, with slicers for year, state and source.

04 —The insights
Finding 01

Output grew about 40%, with one visible break in 2009.

Annual generation rose from roughly 8.8M to 12.3M GWh. The only real interruption is the 2009 recession dip, after which growth resumed.

Finding 02

Only 16% of the power came from renewables.

38.0M of 240M GWh. Non-renewable sources still supplied roughly 84%, so a national “progress” headline hides how far there is to go.

Finding 03

A handful of grids set the national trend.

Texas generates the most electricity by a wide margin and carries the heaviest CO₂ footprint. Washington leads renewables, ahead of California and Texas, largely on hydropower.

05 —The impact

What changed, and what I recommended.

16%renewable share, the headline number
+40%annual output, 2001 → 2023
Texastop generator and top emitter
  1. Track renewable share by state, not just nationally: the national 16% hides very different state-level progress.
  2. Pair generation with emissions intensity (CO₂ per GWh) to separate high-output states from high-polluting ones.
  3. Use Washington and California as benchmarks when modelling transition paths for high-output states like Texas.
